\begin{abstract}
We study a completed full-parameter supervised fine-tuning run of Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct using 15,000 examples selected from the assignment's AceReason corpus. The selection procedure combines answer-completeness and repetition filters with topic clustering and lexical reasoning-route coverage. We compare the resulting model with the original base on nine reasoning benchmarks using identical sampling and grading settings: eight responses per question, temperature 0.6, top-$p$ 0.95, and up to 32,768 generated tokens within the configured context window. On the assignment's seven-benchmark average, pass@8 increases from 49.55\% to 51.52\%, while pass@1 decreases from 23.55\% to 21.85\%. The recorded paired confidence interval for the pass@8 difference includes zero. Truncated samples increase from 141 to 1,964 among 15,792 responses per model. These findings indicate that the apparent average gain depends on the evaluation metric and accompanies substantially worse stopping behavior. We document the selection algorithm, actual 4,096-token training context, training dynamics, per-benchmark uncertainty, and limitations of attributing the result to data selection without a matched random-data training control.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Supervised fine-tuning provides a direct way to adapt an instruction-following language model to mathematical and scientific reasoning demonstrations. In a fixed training-data budget, the selection of examples is an important experimental choice: selecting many near-duplicate solutions may offer little topic coverage, whereas prioritizing only difficult or long demonstrations may create a mismatch between the available training context and the responses being taught. The present project examines a practical selection pipeline and reports the behavior of its completed model rather than assuming that longer solutions or lower training loss imply better reasoning.

The starting model is Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct, an instruction-tuned causal language model with approximately 3.09 billion parameters~\cite{qwen}. Training uses LLaMA-Factory~\cite{llamafactory} and a 15,000-example subset derived from the assignment's AceReason-100K source~\cite{acereason,assignment}. The base and fine-tuned models are evaluated on identical questions and under matching generation settings. This comparison measures the combined effect of the selected examples and the stated training configuration; it does not separately identify the causal contribution of each selection component.

The report addresses three questions. First, how can source-text features be used to obtain a topic-balanced and stylistically varied subset? Second, how does the completed model perform on each benchmark and on the assignment's specified average? Third, does performance under multiple sampling agree with single-sample accuracy and response termination? These questions motivate reporting the full score table, paired intervals, training traces, and truncation behavior together.

\section{Data and Selection Method}
\subsection{Corpus Preparation}
The input is the supplied AceReason-100K dataset~\cite{acereason}. Records are normalized into an instruction--response representation with optional input and system fields. The preparation code removes invalid records, deduplicates normalized prompts, and excludes matching benchmark questions. Near-overlap filtering uses normalized character shingles with a minimum-overlap criterion. Validation examples are reserved before selection, and near-duplicates of validation questions are removed from the candidate pool.

The resulting random and advanced exports each contain 15,000 examples. The random export samples from the prepared candidate pool with seed 42. The advanced export applies additional eligibility and coverage criteria. Consequently, the two exports differ in both filtering and sampling; they should not be treated as differing solely in cluster allocation.

\subsection{Eligibility Filters}
Token counts are measured using the Qwen chat template. An advanced-selection example must have at least 128 response tokens, a detected complete boxed answer, and a repeated-word-eight-gram fraction no larger than 0.35. The full serialized sequence must fit the 8,192-token selection limit with a 32-token reserve. The repeated-eight-gram fraction is
\begin{equation}
 r(y)=1-\frac{|\operatorname{unique}(G_8(y))|}{|G_8(y)|},
\end{equation}
where $G_8(y)$ is the sequence of contiguous word eight-grams; the value is defined as zero when no such eight-grams exist. These filters measure observable properties of demonstrations. A boxed answer and low repetition do not certify mathematical correctness.

\subsection{Topic Coverage and Route Features}
Topic features are obtained from prompt text. The default implementation uses character TF--IDF features, truncated singular-value decomposition, and MiniBatch K-means clustering. For the selected advanced export, the quota table contains 113 populated eligible clusters. A greedy square-root allocation balances corpus prevalence and broader coverage. If cluster $j$ has capacity $N_j$ and current quota $q_j$, each additional slot is assigned to the non-full cluster maximizing
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\sqrt{N_j}}{q_j+1}, \qquad \sum_j q_j=15{,}000.
\end{equation}
This allocation reduces the dominance of very large clusters while respecting each cluster's available examples.

Within each cluster, coverage is measured using a 180-dimensional lexical route vector. Twelve cue families cover checking, revision, alternative approaches, case reasoning, conclusions, modular arithmetic, geometry, counting and probability, algebra, calculus, invariants and proof techniques, and enumeration. The vector concatenates normalized cue counts, the fraction of each cue appearing in the latter half of the response, normalized cue-to-cue transition counts, and cue-presence indicators. This representation describes solution text rather than an inferred proof graph.

The vectors are standardized over the eligible corpus, clipped to the range $[-5,5]$, and normalized before a greedy farthest-point procedure. The first example maximizes a low-repetition quality score. Subsequent choices combine distance to the nearest selected example with quality:
\begin{equation}
 U_i=0.8\min\!\left(\frac{d_i}{2},1\right)+0.2(1-r(y_i)),
\end{equation}
where $d_i$ is the nearest squared distance in normalized route-feature space. Selected examples are shuffled with seed 42. The saved response-token totals are approximately 62.87 million for the advanced export and 109.50 million for the random export. These are export-level counts, not counts of tokens actually supervised by the trainer.

\section{Training Setup}
\subsection{Objective and Configuration}
Training updates all model parameters using the response-only autoregressive cross-entropy objective. For input $x$, target response $y$, and the set $\mathcal{S}$ of supervised response positions retained after preprocessing,
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal{L}(\theta)=-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{S}}
 \log p_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t}).
\end{equation}
Prompt positions are excluded from the loss. The run uses the Qwen template, BF16 mixed precision, SDPA attention and gradient checkpointing. Packing is disabled. Table~\ref{tab:train} lists the actual configuration of the submitted model.

\subsection{Context and Loader Considerations}
The 8,192-token data-selection cutoff differs from the actual 4,096-token training context. Eligible demonstrations can therefore pass selection but be truncated during training, potentially removing part of the final derivation or answer. The exported dataset should not be described as 8,192 tokens of supervised context per example. The original dataset configuration also does not explicitly map the system column. The report retains these details as limitations of this completed run; it does not infer a measured causal effect from either mismatch.

\section{Evaluation Protocol}
\subsection{Benchmarks and Generation}
The benchmark suite contains AIME 2024, AIME 2025, MATH-500, CN Math 2024, Kaoyan, AMC, Minerva Math, OlympiadBench and GPQA-Diamond. The recorded suite contains 1,974 records. Each model generates eight answers per record, yielding 15,792 sampled responses per model. AIME 2025 was separately sourced as a supplement to the supplied suite. The MATH evaluation here contains 500 records; reported denominators use the actual evaluated records rather than the count printed in the assignment's introductory table.

Both arms use temperature 0.6, top-$p$ 0.95, at most 32,768 new tokens, a configured maximum model length of 32,768, seed 0 with stable question-specific seeds, batch size 1,024, at most 128 active sequences, tensor parallelism one and GPU-memory utilization 0.85 on one B200. The system instruction requests step-by-step reasoning and a boxed final answer. Answer extraction and rule-based grading follow the assignment implementation~\cite{assignment}. The model context includes the prompt, so the number of available output tokens can be smaller than the new-token limit.

\subsection{Metrics and Aggregation}
Let $c_i$ be the number of correct sampled responses among $n=8$ for question $i$. The recorded pass@1 is the average sample correctness, while pass@8 is the fraction of records for which at least one sample is correct:
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{pass@1}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{c_i}{8},
 \qquad
 \operatorname{pass@8}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}[c_i>0].
\end{equation}
The assignment's AVG is an unweighted mean over seven benchmarks: AIME 2025, CN Math 2024, Kaoyan, AMC, Minerva Math, OlympiadBench and GPQA. AIME 2024 and MATH are reported individually but are not part of that average. This mean is distinct from pooling all questions across datasets or averaging all nine benchmarks.

\subsection{Paired Uncertainty}
Saved per-benchmark intervals use a paired bootstrap with 2,000 resamples and seed 42. Questions, rather than individual sampled answers, form the resampling units. Repeated normalized question records are clustered together while preserving their record weights. The same resampled units are used for both models. The intervals are descriptive, per-comparison intervals without a multiple-comparison correction, and they do not measure variation across training seeds. Table~\ref{tab:intervals} lists the recorded per-benchmark intervals.

Kaoyan and GPQA also show higher pass@8 after fine-tuning. Conversely, AIME 2024 and AMC decrease, while MATH, Minerva and OlympiadBench change slightly downward. The observed result is therefore not a uniform performance gain. The assignment mean reflects the weighting rule applied to these mixed changes rather than a general improvement on every reasoning domain.

The recorded paired interval for the seven-benchmark pass@8 difference is $[-1.89,+5.74]$ percentage points. Because it contains zero, the experiment does not establish a statistically conclusive average pass@8 gain. The corresponding recorded pass@1 interval is $[-3.16,-0.22]$ points for a mean difference of $-1.70$ points. These intervals describe uncertainty conditional on the completed runs and the evaluation samples, not the uncertainty of retraining the entire method.

\section{Generation Behavior and Interpretation}
The multiple-sample metric and single-sample metric move in different directions. Average pass@1 decreases from 23.55\% to 21.85\%, even though average pass@8 increases. A model can become more likely to produce at least one correct result across several attempts without increasing the expected correctness of a randomly chosen attempt. This experiment records that tradeoff, but it does not identify its mechanism: diversity of reasoning paths, answer variability, token-budget use and sampling effects were not separately manipulated.

Termination changes substantially. The base model has 141 truncated responses across the full suite, compared with 1,964 for the fine-tuned model: approximately 0.89\% and 12.44\% of the 15,792 sampled responses. Truncation measures responses recorded as reaching a length boundary. It does not by itself establish whether those responses would eventually become correct, nor does it account for all computational cost.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/generation_tradeoffs.pdf}
\caption{Generation behavior and the metric tradeoff. Left: truncated samples as a percentage of sampled responses within each benchmark. Right: the specified seven-benchmark mean for pass@1 and pass@8. The panels describe the same completed evaluation rather than a separate experiment.}\label{fig:tradeoffs}

The sharp increase in truncation is relevant to usability: a higher multiple-attempt solve rate does not guarantee efficient or dependable individual answers. Training on longer source responses while restricting the training context to 4,096 tokens is a plausible concern, but the present evidence does not isolate that factor. It would be inappropriate to attribute the observed termination behavior solely to truncation, the loader mapping, the selection rule or a particular hyperparameter without a controlled comparison.

\section{Limitations and Reproducibility}
\textbf{No matched random-data training control.} Both random and advanced datasets are distributed, but a completed full-parameter random-data model evaluated under this same nine-benchmark protocol is not available. The base-versus-SFT comparison measures a complete training pipeline, not the advantage of advanced selection over random selection. A fair selection ablation would hold initialization, training budget, context, optimizer, sampling and grading fixed while varying the selected data.

\textbf{Single training seed and adaptive exploration.} The report concerns one completed training seed. Benchmark resampling does not substitute for multiple training seeds. The model comes from a broader project with other experiments, so the present evaluation should not be treated as a preregistered confirmation study.

\textbf{Proxy-based selection.} Route vectors detect lexical patterns, not the validity or causal structure of reasoning. Quality filters favor complete-looking answers and low repetition but cannot guarantee correctness. Cluster quotas also depend on the chosen text representation and clustering procedure. The experiment does not establish that these particular design choices are optimal.

\textbf{Data and metric boundaries.} Overlap removal reduces recognizable contamination but does not prove absence of all semantic overlap or exclude pretraining exposure. AIME 2025 is separately sourced, and results should be interpreted using the actual evaluated records. Rule-based grading and boxed-answer extraction can also differ from human judgments of reasoning quality.

\textbf{Artifacts.} The \href{https://github.com/rohitsalla/reasoning-sft-submission}{public project repository} includes the configuration, selection/evaluation code, recorded scores, per-question correctness vectors, this LaTeX source and plot-generation script. The model and both 15,000-example datasets are hosted on Hugging Face. The result-checking script recomputes all benchmark scores from the saved correctness vectors. The public download script uses a readable release tag. Original model, dataset and software license terms remain applicable.

\section{Conclusion}
The completed 15,000-example full-parameter SFT run produces a mixed reasoning result. The seven-benchmark pass@8 mean rises numerically to 51.52\%, but its recorded confidence interval does not establish a conclusive gain over the base. Average pass@1 declines, and length-limited outputs become substantially more common. Reporting these outcomes together is essential: average multi-attempt accuracy alone would obscure a material change in individual-answer reliability and generation behavior. A matched random-data control, consistent selection/training context and repeated training seeds are the most direct next experiments for evaluating whether the proposed selection procedure offers a reproducible benefit.
